---
title: "Why Lotus exists"
description: "Prediction market liquidity is fragmented. Lotus unifies it."
---

Prediction market liquidity is fragmented.

The same real-world event can trade across several venues at the same time. Each venue can have different prices, orderbook depth, wallet requirements, fees, market rules, resolution sources, and settlement timelines.

That creates a hard problem for traders and builders.

## The user problem

A trader who wants exposure to one outcome often has to answer several questions before placing a trade:

- Which venue has the best price?
- Which venue has enough depth for my size?
- Are the markets actually the same?
- Which market resolves from which source?
- Do I have the right wallet or venue balance?
- Will the price move before I can submit?
- Will my position show correctly after the fill?

Most users should not have to solve that manually.

Lotus gives users one canonical market and routes underneath.

## The builder problem

Apps that want to add prediction market trading face the same fragmentation.

Without Lotus, a builder needs to integrate each venue, maintain market mappings, compare rules, handle wallets, manage orderbooks, track execution status, and reconcile positions.

Lotus packages that into an API, routing layer, and execution system.

## The market maker problem

Market makers also face fragmented flow.

They can quote one venue at a time, but user demand is spread across many venues and apps. Lotus creates a structured RFQ path where eligible LPs can receive canonical market demand and compete to improve execution.

## The Lotus thesis

1. Prediction markets will keep multiplying.
2. Liquidity will stay fragmented.
3. Users will prefer one market over a venue picker.
4. Builders will prefer one integration over many venue integrations.
5. Market makers will prefer structured flow over scattered integrations.
6. Execution must stay strict, verifiable, and fail-closed.

## Why this belongs in the execution layer

Aggregation alone is not enough.

A frontend can show many prices, but it cannot safely decide whether two markets are equivalent, whether venue readiness is valid, whether a token ID is executable, or whether a route should be submitted.

Lotus moves that work to the backend. The frontend can stay clean, while execution remains governed by fresh quotes, routeability checks, signer requirements, idempotency, and settlement verification.
